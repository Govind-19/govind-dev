for the past two years i thought i was becoming a good full stack developer.

and on paper.... i was. real features. production bugs. a school operating system running in actual schools. auth, payment workflows, student promotion logic, firestore migrations, dashboards, UI work, a lot of backend APIs.

whenever i got stuck, AI helped me understand things faster. i could build features much quicker than before. so i slowly started believing i was ready for product-based company interviews 🤷‍♂️

then came one interview.

i went in expecting system design. architecture. scalability. the product i had actually built.

instead they started with the basics.

javascript. react. node. closures. execution context. the event loop. middleware. http.

things i had used a hundred times.... and couldn't explain confidently 🥲

<Pull>i had become good at building software. i wasn't equally good at explaining why it worked.</Pull>

that interview wasn't unfair. it showed me exactly where i was weak.

for a day i felt disappointed. i kept thinking — *i've built so many real features, why couldn't i answer these questions?*

then it landed. the interview wasn't testing how many features i had shipped. it was testing whether i understood the foundation underneath every one of them. AI had helped me ship. somewhere in there, i stopped building the base.

<Ex>Ex: i could write a debounce. i couldn't explain the closure that makes the timer survive between keystrokes. using it and understanding it are not the same skill.</Ex>

so now i'm going back to the beginning. not because i have to — because i want to be the kind of engineer who understands every layer of the stack.

```text
javascript, from zero
  execution context · scope · closures · hoisting
  promises · async/await · the event loop

then node
  http · tcp · dns
  express · middleware · jwt · the request lifecycle
```

not memorising definitions. understanding what actually happens inside the computer when that line runs.

i've also changed how i use AI. earlier i mostly asked it for solutions. now i ask it to teach me — and i keep asking *why* until i can explain the thing in my own words with nothing in front of me.

<Pull>if i can't explain it simply, i assume i haven't understood it yet.</Pull>

the one part i'm proud of is that i accepted the weakness instead of hiding it. it's easy to say "the interview was difficult". it's harder to say *i wasn't prepared in the areas that mattered*. but that second sentence is the only one that gives you a direction.

this interview didn't reduce my confidence. it corrected it.

i still build software every day. the difference is that every feature is now also a chance to understand what's happening underneath it.

maybe this wasn't a failure. maybe it was the point where i stopped trying to become someone who can build applications.... and started becoming an engineer who understands them.

this is just the beginning 🙂
